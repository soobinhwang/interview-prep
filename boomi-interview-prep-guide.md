### Pattern 1. Validation & Iteration — Your Weakest Spot Across Every Interview

This came up in every single retro. At CadMakers, you flagged it yourself as "the weakest moment — one of the most important questions in any design interview." At Cova Technical, almost every answer about measuring success falls back on "proxy signals," "CS feedback," and "directional indicators." At OpusClip, when pushed on whether you got enough information, the answer leans on the CS team doing the research for you.

**The problem:** Your answers sound like you're defending a limitation instead of owning a method. Phrases like "while it wasn't perfect," "in a more ideal setup," and "it's not as rich as moderated user research" signal doubt in your own process. Interviewers hear: she knows this wasn't enough.

**What to do tomorrow:** Stop apologizing for proxy signals. Reframe them as a deliberate research strategy. Say: "I designed a lightweight validation loop using three data sources — CS patterns from 100+ tickets, internal behavioral data, and heuristic review. The 50% click reduction post-launch confirmed we'd identified the right problem." No qualifiers. No "it wasn't perfect." The results speak.

**Boomi questions where this will come up:**
- "Walk me through your design process" (the Test/Iterate part)
- "How do you approach designing for scalability?" (how do you know it's working?)
- Any design challenge scenario (they'll ask "how would you validate that?")

### Pattern 4. Answers Ramble When You Should Answer First, Elaborate Second

OpusClip flagged "some answers were unfocused/rambling" and "time management (presentation ran long)." CadMakers noted the evaluators were likely asking "can she be sharper in how she communicates decisions?" Your answers in the retros are thorough — sometimes too thorough. Many are 4-5 sentences when 2-3 would land harder.

**The problem:** In a technical interview, long answers signal uncertainty. When you know something cold, you say it in two sentences. When you're unsure, you keep talking hoping to land on something good. Interviewers notice.

**What to do tomorrow:** Use this structure for every answer:
1. **Answer the question in 1-2 sentences** (the headline)
2. **Stop.** Let them react.
3. **Elaborate with a specific example only if they ask or if the silence invites it**

Practice this especially for: "Walk me through your design process" (don't go through all 6 steps unless asked), "Tell me about yourself" (60-90 seconds max), and any question where you catch yourself saying "and also..."

# PART 2: Scripted Answers for the 5 Highest-Risk Questions
Practice these out loud tonight. Each answer is designed to be 60–90 seconds spoken.

---

## 1. "How did you validate your designs?"

> I use a multi-source validation approach — I don't rely on a single signal. On the storefront redesign at CarltonOne, I triangulated across three inputs: behavioral patterns from 100+ support tickets through our CS team, internal usage data, and heuristic review against competitive benchmarks. That gave me enough confidence to commit to a direction.
>
> Then I validated through iterative prototyping — I'd build lightweight prototypes, review them cross-functionally with engineering and product, and refine based on what we learned before shipping. Post-launch, the results confirmed the approach: 50% reduction in navigation clicks and 40% increase in featured brand engagement.
>
> For Boomi, I'd bring that same resourcefulness — but I'd also be excited to layer in more direct user testing, especially with something like prototype testing with 3–5 users on task completion before committing to a full build. That combination of quantitative signals and direct user feedback is where I want to keep growing.

**Fixes pattern #1 (validation) and #5 (constraints as growth):** No qualifiers. No "it wasn't perfect." Leads with method, lands on results, and bridges to Boomi with a growth framing instead of an excuse.

---

## 2. "What's your experience with AI tools?"

> I'll give you three concrete things. First, I led an 8-week AI workshop at CarltonOne where I upskilled our entire design team on integrating AI into their workflows — that wasn't a side project, it changed how the team operated. Second, I built a Figma plugin that automated part of our design-to-development handoff. Third, I actively prototype in working code using Claude Code and Cursor — I can validate five different interaction patterns in the time it used to take for one static mockup.
>
> What I've learned from all of this is that AI is most valuable when it accelerates the feedback loop, not just the output. You ship faster, you learn faster.
>
> That's actually why Boomi's direction with AI agents is so interesting to me — designing for a platform where users are building and monitoring autonomous workflows is a new kind of UX challenge. How do you give someone meaningful control and visibility over something that's running on its own? That's the kind of problem I want to be solving.

**Fixes pattern #2 (passive AI) and #3 (no company bridge):** Three artifacts in the first 15 seconds. No "I'm interested in" or "I've been experimenting with" — just what you built and what you learned. The Boomi bridge is specific to their AI agent product.

---

## 3. "Walk me through your design process."

> At a high level: understand the problem, explore solutions, build and test, then iterate. But the real answer is that my process flexes based on the project.
>
> Let me give you a specific example. On the storefront redesign at CarltonOne, the ask was vague — "improve the shopping experience." So I started by mapping the existing flow and documenting what we didn't know. I created a structured framework — open questions, design considerations, and user research questions — and used that to align stakeholders on the actual problem before I touched any UI.
>
> From there, I collaborated with engineering early to size solutions by effort and impact, prototyped the top candidates, and iterated through cross-functional reviews. We shipped in phases to reduce risk — starting with new clients, then expanding.
>
> Two things I'd highlight: I bring engineers in during ideation, not at handoff. And I increasingly use AI tools to prototype in working code, which means I can test real interactions faster than static mocks allow. For a product as complex as Boomi's, that speed matters — especially when you're designing for workflows that need to feel intuitive for both developers and business users.

**Fixes pattern #4 (rambling) and #3 (no bridge):** Doesn't robotically list 6 steps. Tells a story, shows collaboration, mentions AI naturally, and bridges to Boomi's complexity. Aim for 90 seconds max — if they want more, they'll ask.

---

## 4. "How would you design [X] for Boomi?" (Design Challenge)

Use this framework no matter what they ask you to design:

> **Step 1 — Clarify (15 seconds):**
> "Before I sketch anything, I'd want to clarify a few things: Who's the primary user — is this for a developer building integrations, or a business user using no-code tools? What's the core job they're trying to do? And what does success look like — is it speed, accuracy, confidence?"
>
> **Step 2 — Frame the problem (30 seconds):**
> "Based on [their answer], the core design tension here is [X vs Y]. For example, if it's a dashboard monitoring 1,000 AI agents — the tension is between comprehensive visibility and actionable focus. Users don't need to see 1,000 things at once; they need to see the 3 things that need attention right now."
>
> **Step 3 — Sketch a direction (60 seconds):**
> "I'd start with [specific approach]. For the agent dashboard example: a status-first view — healthy agents are collapsed into a summary count, while agents that need attention surface to the top with clear status indicators and recommended actions. Progressive disclosure: summary → detail → action. That way a business user can scan health at a glance, while a developer can drill into logs and configuration."
>
> **Step 4 — Talk trade-offs (30 seconds):**
> "The trade-off here is between simplicity and control. If we over-simplify, power users feel limited. If we expose everything, new users feel overwhelmed. I'd solve that with role-based defaults — business users see the simplified view by default, developers see the detailed view — but both can switch."
>
> **Step 5 — Validation (15 seconds, and this is where you usually stumble):**
> "To validate, I'd run a prototype test with 3–5 users from each persona — give them a realistic scenario like 'one of your agents just failed, find it and fix it,' measure task completion time and confidence, and iterate before committing to the full build."

**Fixes pattern #1 (validation) and #3 (bridge):** Shows structured thinking without being robotic. The validation step is specific and confident — no "I'd talk to CS" or "we'd use proxy signals." You name the method, the user count, the task, and the metric.

---

## 5. "Tell me about a time you simplified something complex."

> The best example is the reward redemption flow at CarltonOne. The platform served 500+ enterprise clients with a massive product catalog — merchandise, gift cards, tickets, experiences — and users couldn't find what they were looking for. The existing flow buried categories behind 4 clicks, and only 15% of users ever discovered the featured brands section.
>
> I restructured the information architecture: exposed the top-level taxonomy directly in a mega-menu, surfaced featured brands at the first level instead of burying them, and designed one flexible template that worked across different content types — so merchandise and gift cards used the same layout pattern instead of separate designs.
>
> The result: 50% fewer clicks to reach products, 40% increase in featured brand engagement, and the engineering team shipped it faster because one template replaced multiple custom patterns.
>
> This is actually the same design challenge Boomi faces at a different scale — making a complex integration platform approachable without dumbing it down. The principle is the same: expose what matters most, use progressive disclosure for depth, and design systems rather than one-off screens.

**Fixes pattern #3 (no bridge) and #4 (rambling):** Tight story with clear before/after and three metrics. The Boomi bridge at the end is specific — it names their actual problem (complex integration, approachability) and connects your principle to their context.

---

## 6. "How do you ensure design consistency and user-centered design?"

> I treat these as two sides of the same system — consistency is how you scale user-centered decisions, and user-centered thinking is what keeps consistency from becoming rigid.

> At CarltonOne, I built the design system from scratch specifically because inconsistency was hurting the user experience. We had a white-label platform serving 500+ clients, and every new client setup introduced slight UI variations — different spacing, different button styles, different interaction patterns. Users across clients were getting a fractured experience, and the engineering team was spending time on one-off fixes instead of building features.
>
> So I mapped every repeating pattern, created a shared component library with documented usage guidelines, and partnered with the lead developer to define specs so engineering and design were working from the same source of truth. I also set up governance — who proposes new components, how we review them, and how we document decisions. That eliminated manual redlining entirely and reduced feature delivery time by 33%.
>
> But the key part is: the system was built from user needs, not just visual tidiness. Every component decision started with "what does the user need here?" not "what looks consistent?" For example, when we debated the mega-menu pattern, I grounded the decision in user behavior data — 85% of users navigated through the first two category levels — rather than just picking what matched our existing dropdown style.
>
> For Boomi, this matters even more because you have 10+ products that need to feel like one platform. The challenge isn't just visual consistency — it's making sure a business user who learned the pattern in one Boomi product can transfer that knowledge to another without re-learning. That's where a design system becomes a user experience strategy, not just a component library.

**Fixes pattern #1 (validation — grounds decisions in data), #3 (Boomi bridge), and #4 (concise structure):** Opens with a clear principle, tells the CarltonOne story with specifics, then bridges to Boomi's multi-product challenge. The key differentiator: frames design systems as a user experience strategy, not just visual consistency.
