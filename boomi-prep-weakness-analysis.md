## The 5 Recurring Patterns Holding You Back

### 1. Validation & Iteration — Your Weakest Spot Across Every Interview

This came up in every single retro. At CadMakers, you flagged it yourself as "the weakest moment — one of the most important questions in any design interview." At Cova Technical, almost every answer about measuring success falls back on "proxy signals," "CS feedback," and "directional indicators." At OpusClip, when pushed on whether you got enough information, the answer leans on the CS team doing the research for you.

**The problem:** Your answers sound like you're defending a limitation instead of owning a method. Phrases like "while it wasn't perfect," "in a more ideal setup," and "it's not as rich as moderated user research" signal doubt in your own process. Interviewers hear: she knows this wasn't enough.

**What to do tomorrow:** Stop apologizing for proxy signals. Reframe them as a deliberate research strategy. Say: "I designed a lightweight validation loop using three data sources — CS patterns from 100+ tickets, internal behavioral data, and heuristic review. The 50% click reduction post-launch confirmed we'd identified the right problem." No qualifiers. No "it wasn't perfect." The results speak.

**Boomi questions where this will come up:**
- "Walk me through your design process" (the Test/Iterate part)
- "How do you approach designing for scalability?" (how do you know it's working?)
- Any design challenge scenario (they'll ask "how would you validate that?")

### 2. AI Answers Sound Interested, Not Practiced

CadMakers flagged this directly: "AI question read as passive — for a JD that explicitly lists AI fluency as a requirement, that's a meaningful gap." Your current AI answers across retros say things like "I've been actively experimenting," "I'm especially interested in teams that..." and "I'm interested in teams where AI is applied thoughtfully." These are interest statements, not competence statements.

**The problem:** You have real AI credentials — an 8-week workshop you led, a Figma plugin you built, active use of Claude Code and Cursor for prototyping. But your answers bury the concrete stuff under soft, aspirational language. For an AI product company like Boomi, this is the difference between "she's curious about AI" and "she's already shipping with AI."

**What to do tomorrow:** Lead with the artifact, not the interest. Say: "I led an 8-week AI workshop that upskilled our entire design team. I built a Figma plugin that automated part of our workflow. And right now I prototype in working code using Claude Code — I can validate five interaction patterns in the time it used to take for one mockup. That's not theoretical; it's how I actually work." Then connect to Boomi: "For an AI agent platform, I'd want to understand how designers prototype agent behaviors before the models are production-ready."

**Boomi questions where this will come up:**
- "What's your experience with AI tools or AI-powered features?" (almost guaranteed)
- "Walk me through your design process" (mention AI in the prototyping phase)
- Any design challenge (use AI as part of your approach, not just a footnote)

### 3. You Don't Connect Your Experience to THEIR Specific Problem

OpusClip retro: "Missed opportunities to connect experience to OpusClip's needs." CadMakers retro: "Never connected my experience to their specific UX challenge (power vs. accessibility for non-specialist users)." This is a pattern — you tell the CarltonOne story well, but you stop at your story. You don't bridge to "and here's exactly how this maps to what you're building."

**The problem:** Interviewers aren't just evaluating whether you've done good work. They're evaluating whether you can do good work HERE. Every answer that ends with your CarltonOne outcome but doesn't say "and this is why it matters for Boomi" is a missed opportunity.

**What to do tomorrow:** End every major answer with a one-sentence Boomi bridge. Examples:
- After talking about templates/scalability: "...which maps directly to Boomi's challenge of maintaining design consistency across 10+ products."
- After talking about admin vs. employee personas: "...which is the same tension Boomi navigates between developer users building integrations and business users using no-code tools."
- After talking about the design system: "...and I'd be excited to contribute to a system that needs to scale across Boomi's product suite."

**Boomi questions where this will come up:** Literally every question. Make the bridge a habit.

### 4. Answers Ramble When You Should Answer First, Elaborate Second

OpusClip flagged "some answers were unfocused/rambling" and "time management (presentation ran long)." CadMakers noted the evaluators were likely asking "can she be sharper in how she communicates decisions?" Your answers in the retros are thorough — sometimes too thorough. Many are 4-5 sentences when 2-3 would land harder.

**The problem:** In a technical interview, long answers signal uncertainty. When you know something cold, you say it in two sentences. When you're unsure, you keep talking hoping to land on something good. Interviewers notice.

**What to do tomorrow:** Use this structure for every answer:
1. **Answer the question in 1-2 sentences** (the headline)
2. **Stop.** Let them react.
3. **Elaborate with a specific example only if they ask or if the silence invites it**

Practice this especially for: "Walk me through your design process" (don't go through all 6 steps unless asked), "Tell me about yourself" (60-90 seconds max), and any question where you catch yourself saying "and also..."

### 5. You Frame Constraints as Limitations Instead of Growth Motivation

CadMakers retro nailed this: you sound like "a designer who did her best in a constrained environment" instead of "a designer who already has the ambiguity, systems, and collaboration muscles this role needs, and is now specifically looking for an environment with closer customer access and a more mature product loop."

Across your answers, phrases like "direct user access was one of the biggest constraints," "in a more ideal setup," and "it's not as rich as moderated user research" make constraints sound like excuses. Cova's feedback wanted "more detailed stories of going back and forth with developers" — but your answers about engineering collaboration stay high-level ("I bring engineers in early") without the messy, specific back-and-forth that makes stories believable.

**What to do tomorrow:** Two shifts:
1. When talking about research constraints, say: "I built a multi-source validation approach that worked within our context — and the results confirmed it. But one reason Boomi excites me is the opportunity to pair that resourcefulness with deeper customer access."
2. When talking about engineering collaboration, give ONE specific back-and-forth moment: "I proposed exposing all category levels, the developer flagged a performance concern, so we scoped it to two levels and shipped in half the time. That specific trade-off conversation is what I mean by early collaboration."

## Priority Questions to Drill Tonight

Based on these patterns, here are the questions most likely to expose these weaknesses at Boomi tomorrow, ranked by risk:

### HIGH RISK — Practice These Out Loud

1. **"How did you validate your designs?"** — Your #1 weakness. Practice a confident, no-qualifiers version.

2. **"What's your experience with AI tools?"** — Lead with artifacts (workshop, plugin, Claude Code), not interest. Then bridge to Boomi's AI agent platform.

3. **"Walk me through your design process."** — Keep it to 90 seconds. Don't walk through all 6 steps. Hit the highlights, mention collaboration and AI prototyping, stop.

4. **"How would you design [X] for Boomi?"** (design challenge) — When they ask "how would you validate?" don't default to "I'd talk to CS." Say: "I'd run a prototype test with 3-5 users, measure task completion, and iterate before committing to full build."

5. **"Tell me about a time you simplified something complex."** — Good story with the redemption flow, but bridge to Boomi: "Making a complex integration tool approachable for non-technical users is the same design problem at a different scale."

### MEDIUM RISK — Know Your Talking Points

6. **"How do you work with engineers?"** — Give one specific trade-off conversation, not just "I bring them in early."

7. **"How do you handle designing for multiple user personas?"** — Bridge hard to Boomi's developer vs. business user tension.

8. **"Tell me about your design system experience."** — Talk about governance, not just creation. Boomi has 10+ products — they care about how it scales.

### LOWER RISK — Your Strengths (Don't Overthink These)

9. **"Tell me about a complex B2B product you worked on."** — This is your home court. Just keep it concise.

10. **"How do you balance user needs with business goals?"** — Wallet story is strong. Just add the Boomi bridge at the end.
